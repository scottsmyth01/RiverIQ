## What is Equity?

Understanding equity is one of the most important fundamentals to understand and wrap your head around. There are many articles online which explain equity, but the topic tends to get convoluted. Simply put, equity is the % chance you have of winning the pot if all community cards are dealt. For example, if your hand has 70% equity in a pot, it means that if the exact same situation occurred thousands of times, you would expect to win approximately 70% of those hands. There is one caveat however. As more cards are dealt your equity changes. A common scenario is when you have top pair on the flop, but the turn brings a possible flush and/or straight draw that your opponent may have connected with.

Example:

Hero: A♥K♥, Villain: 5♣3♣
Flop: A♠ 6♣ 4♣

Before the flop, hero is a favorite to win the pot, with a raw equity of around 61.4% leaving villain with a 38% chance to take down the pot. However, everything changes once the flop comes down giving villain a combo draw (flush + straight). Villain now has a 57% chance to win the pot, quite a jump! What is important to keep in mind is that equity can fluctuate, and just because you have a great pre-flop equity does not mean that this gets maintained as the hand progresses.

## Factors Affecting Equity

Several factors influence a hand’s equity throughout a poker hand.

**Number of opponents**

The number of opponents has a significant impact. Premium hands perform exceptionally well heads-up, but their equity decreases as more players enter the pot. Pocket aces remain the best starting hand in Texas Hold’em, but they become less likely to win against multiple opponents.

**Board Texture**

Board texture also plays a major role. A dry board such as A♦ 7♣ 2♠ offers relatively few drawing opportunities, while a coordinated board like 9♠ 8♠ 7♦ creates numerous possible straights and flushes that can dramatically shift equity. You can c-bet more often on dry boards since you will get more folds vs. a wet board where the number of hands that connect with the board is drastically higher.

**Position**

Your position at the table also matters. Acting later in the betting order gives you more information about your opponents’ actions, allowing you to make better decisions and often realize more of your hand’s equity.

**Hand Ranges**

Experienced players think in terms of ranges rather than individual hands. Since you rarely know your opponent’s exact cards, strong poker decisions are made by estimating how your range performs against the range of hands they could realistically hold.

## Expected Value (EV)

Expected value (EV) is the average amount you expect to win or lose if you could repeat the exact same situation thousands of times. This concept can be intimidating at first, but once you wrap your head around some simple examples, it will be easier for you to apply on the felt. Consider this elementary example. You pay me $1 on a coin flip. If it is heads then I give you $2 and if it's tails, then you get $0. There are only two outcomes, either you win, or you lose. Now is when it gets a little tricky. If we run this hypothetical situation 100 times, we can assume that your chance of winning is 50%.

Your Wins: 50 wins \* $2 = $100
Your Costs: 100 games \* $1 = $100

So after 100 games, you made $100 and spent $100, leaving you with a net profit of $0 - neither player has an advantage.

Great, so now let's build on this example. Suppose that instead of $2, I give you $3 if it is heads. Now the math would look something like this:

Your Wins: 50 wins \* $3 = $150
Your Costs: 100 games \* $1 = $100

Now, after 100 games we made $150 and spent $100, leaving you with a nice profit of $50 ($0.50 per game). This is positive EV (+EV). If we re-ran this exmaple where instead I give you $1.50 per win, you would get something like this:

Your Wins: 50 wins \* $1.50 = $75
Your Costs: 100 games \* $1 = $100

This would leave you with an EV of -$25 (-$0.25 every game). This is negative EV (-EV).

## Pot Odds

Now, let's consider how this applies on the felt. Every time you are faced with a bet from an opponent, you are paying some value (opponents bet) in order to win some other amount (the pot). Suppose the pot contains $100 and your opponent bets $50. The cost/risk is $50, and the potential award is $150. The odds are simply comparing risk to reward, so in this example we would have 150:50, or 3:1 odds (or 25%). How does this help us make better decisions a the table? If our equity is 25% or more, we can confidently make this call and it would profitable over a large sample of hands.

You will not always have the right odds to call but there is something called implied odds which can make a call +EV over the long run even if we are not getting the right immediate odds. Suppose we are playing against an opponent who loves to bet with most of their range. Suppose the hand looks something like this:

The pot: $20
Opponent bets: $20

In this situation we are risking $20 to win $40, giving us 2:1 odds, not great. However, we have a drawing hand to the flush and know that this opponent is liekly to bet heavily with top pair or better. Now we can re-calculate the odds:

The pot: $20
Opponent bets: $20
Potential profit: $100

Now we are not only playing to win the $20 in the pot, but the extra $100 we expect to win if we hit our flush. Putting all of this together, the general flow is something like:

How much does it cost? -> how much can I win right now? -> Can I win more later? -> Is the call profitable overall?

Or in poker terms:

Cost -> Pot odds -> Implied odds -> Expected Value (EV)

Equity, expected value (EV), and pot odds form the foundation of profitable poker decisions. Instead of asking whether a particular play won or lost this time, start asking whether it was the most profitable decision over thousands of similar situations. Poker is a game of long-term averages, and short-term results are often influenced by variance. By consistently comparing your equity to the odds you’re being offered, and considering implied odds when appropriate, you’ll make better decisions, reduce costly mistakes, and build a fundamentally stronger poker strategy. Master these concepts first, and every advanced topic you learn afterward will become much easier to understand.
